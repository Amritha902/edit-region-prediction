Locating the Edit Region

Forty-five works, 2024–2026, on where an instruction says to edit — and why none of them answers the question

Amritha S 23BEC1368  ·  Yugeshwaran P 23BEC1404

Guide: Dr. Saranya M (54783) · School of Electronics Engineering, VIT Chennai

13 September 2026

Abstract

Instruction-guided image editing has advanced quickly, but almost entirely on the assumption that the region to edit is already known. We survey 45 works published between 2024 and 2026 and find the field divides into those that consume a region and those that read one out of a generator's internals — with exactly one line of work that predicts a region from the instruction itself.

Four gaps follow, and we state what we measured against each rather than asserting them. The sharpest is that no benchmark scores a predicted edit region: the closest, LocateEdit-Bench, performs post-hoc forensics on edits that already happened. That absence is why a 2026 paper whose headline contribution is a MaskPredictor can omit a mask IoU without objection.

1Scope and selection

Papers were included if they bear on where an instruction-guided edit should happen: editors whose localization is implicit, methods that read or consume regions, insertion and affordance work, text-guided segmentation, benchmarks, and the surveys that frame the area. Patents were included where they claim region generation, because the commercial position matters for a novelty argument.

Every work is 2024 or later — a three-year window as of September 2026. Two foundational works fall outside it and are therefore cited as method components rather than as related work: FastSAM (2023), whose YOLACT-style prototypes are the basis our head predicts coefficients over, and LISA (2023), which founded reasoning segmentation. Excluding them from the survey keeps the window honest; pretending we do not build on them would not be.

Each entry carries a relevance score from 1 to 5. A 5 means the work either defines our problem, bounds it, or is the specific thing we must beat; 16 of the 45 qualify and are set in bold in the tables below. All 45 links resolve, and 42 PDFs are archived alongside the matrix.

3Edit localization (7)

This is the cluster our work sits in, and it splits cleanly in two. One half reads a region out of a generator that is already running: Rethinking Where to Edit takes attention cues from the source and target streams, and WhereEdit sequence-matches changed prompt tokens and aggregates cross-attention into a mask. Both are training-free and need no supervision, which is their appeal. Both also inherit the limits of what attention happens to highlight: WhereEdit states outright that it does not handle adding into empty regions, and the attention reader keeps the largest connected component, so repeated instances break it.

The other half consumes a region someone else produced. MaskFlow folds the mask into the probability path of a flow-matching objective, which is elegant precisely because it assumes the mask exists. MADiff is the closest structural precedent — predict a mask, then edit through it — but it is scoped to fashion, and it predicts over garments that are already present.

Our base paper, AdaptEdit, is the only work that trains a predictor to ground the region from the instruction itself. That is our exact formulation. The opening it leaves is not its architecture but its supervision: its masks come from MagicBrush human annotation and rendered text boxes, explicitly not from pixel differences — and human annotation cannot express where a hat should go when there is no hat.

4Instruction editors (9)

Nine works, and the pattern is uniform: they are excellent at editing and silent about localization. Step1X-Edit parses the instruction into editing tokens with an MLLM and decodes with a DiT; Qwen-Image-Edit and FLUX.1 Kontext ship 12–32 B open-weight editors. In each, the decision of which pixels to change is internal, implicit, and never scored.

One work makes the asymmetry explicit. NEP conditions on text tokens, source image tokens and edit-region mask tokens — the region is an input. It consumes a region; it never produces one. Read across the cluster, these papers define the demand side of the problem we are on the supply side of.

5Insertion and affordance (4)

Insertion is the case our project exists for, and this cluster shows the field has converged on solving it given a location. MADD denoises the RGB image and the insertion mask jointly in a dual stream — genuinely predicting mask geometry — but requires a position prompt: you tell it where. Add-it places objects through weighted extended attention with no training at all, so placement emerges rather than being predicted.

The patent literature says the same thing in legal language. US12561956 requires "an original image with a marked region"; US12462449B2 generates per-layer blending masks inside a generator, not a region in empty space. Across research and patents alike, the location is an input.

6Region-aware efficiency (3)

Three works exploit regions to save computation, and all three assume the region arrives from somewhere else. RegionE skips denoising in unchanged areas — but infers the region from an initial denoising pass, i.e. after generation has begun. SpotEdit skips stable regions by perceptual similarity. LazyDiffusion synthesises only the masked region from a compact global context, and requires a user-drawn mask.

This cluster matters to us for a reason beyond motivation: it establishes that a cheap, reusable per-image representation with a cheap per-instruction query is a recognised and valuable structure. That is exactly the split our architecture has, and it is where our latency advantage comes from.

7Text-guided segmentation (5)

The obvious thing to try is a referring segmenter, and this cluster is why that fails. Referring segmentation returns what a phrase denotes. For "put a hat on the man", the phrase denotes the man; the region that must change is the empty space above his head. The referring-segmentation survey makes the distinction we rely on explicit.

We measured the consequence rather than assuming it. CLIPSeg — the standard referring segmenter, 150.7 M parameters — scores 0.1849 IoU overall on our task but only 0.054 on insertions, its worst category and our best margin. VespaSeg is an important precedent in the other direction: LoRA on a MobileSAM decoder proves lightweight text-conditioned segmentation is viable, which is the bet our 4.7 M head makes.

8Benchmarks and metrics (4)

Four works, and none of them scores what we produce. LocateEdit-Bench comes closest by name and is the furthest in substance: 231 K images, three edit types, two protocols — all scoring localization of edits that already occurred. It is post-hoc forensics on a finished edit, not an evaluation of a region predicted beforehand. GEdit-Bench and the region/semantic metric score the output image.

This absence is why our project reports a mask IoU at all, and why the base paper could omit one without reviewers objecting: there is no benchmark whose leaderboard would have exposed it.

9Planning and reasoning (4)

These works decompose hard instructions before editing. X-Planner breaks complex instructions into steps; RePlan adds region-aligned guidance with an MLLM planner. The planning is semantic — what to do, in what order — and stops short of where, in pixels. They also put a multi-billion-parameter model in the loop for a decision our 4.7 M head makes in 5.3 ms.

12The four gaps

The clusters above converge on four specific openings. We state each with the evidence from the literature and with what we measured ourselves, because a gap asserted from reading alone is a weaker claim than one that has been reproduced.

12.1Editing is solved given a region; producing one is not

Clusters on editors, efficiency and flow-matching all perform well when told where to edit. NEP takes the mask as an input; MaskFlow builds the mask into its objective; LazyDiffusion requires a user-drawn one. We tested the obvious substitute and it failed cleanly: selecting a FastSAM mask by CLIP similarity scores 0 of 7 across modify, remove and insert, failing on granularity (returning the whole person for "the jacket"), on spatial language, and on insertion in principle.

12.2The edit region is often not an object

Our base paper states this against itself: "geometry-changing edits with no localized source region ('put a hat on the dog' where the dog has no hat)." WhereEdit says it too — it does not handle adding into empty regions. Referring segmentation cannot help, because it returns what the phrase denotes. Measured: CLIPSeg reaches 0.1849 overall but 0.054 on insertion.

12.3Supervision, not architecture, is the binding constraint

AdaptEdit supervises its MaskPredictor with MagicBrush human annotation and rendered text boxes — explicitly not pixel differences. We measured what that costs. Across all 528 MagicBrush dev turns the human masks cover 62.4% of the frame against a true changed area of 10.3%: 9.1× too large, precision 15.8%, and worst on insertions at 11.9×. They are region-of-interest scribbles, and they are the supervision the field is training on.

12.4Nothing scores a predicted edit region

LocateEdit-Bench scores edits that already occurred. GEdit-Bench and the region/semantic metric score the output image. The affordance benchmark measures plausibility, not predicted-region IoU. There is no leaderboard on which a missing mask IoU would have been noticed — which is precisely how a 2026 paper built around a MaskPredictor reports none.

14Improvement over what is actually comparable

Most works in this survey cannot be compared to ours numerically: they optimise image-quality scores on benchmarks that never expose a region, or they are training-free methods on a different task. Claiming a percentage against them would be meaningless. Four reference points are directly comparable, because we ran them on the same 503 held-out turns with the same ground truth.

The one comparison we cannot make

AdaptEdit is our base paper and the only work with our exact formulation, yet no number in this table is theirs. It reports no mask IoU, and at 20.43 B frozen plus roughly 5.0 B trainable parameters we cannot run it to obtain one. That is not an evasion — it is the gap. We reimplement its mechanism at a scale we can run honestly and supply the measurement it omits.

16What the survey implies for novelty

Reading 45 recent works leaves three defensible positions and one that is not available to us.

Available. Predicting the edit region from the instruction is nearly vacant — two works, one domain-locked. Deriving supervision from pixel differences rather than human annotation is, as far as this survey found, unattempted in this setting, and we measured that it beats the human annotation the field trains on. Reporting a mask IoU at all is novel by absence.

Not available. Claiming novelty for the mechanism. FiLM modulation and a BCE + Dice objective are AdaptEdit's design choices as well as ours; we reached them independently but that is not a claim. Likewise, a lightweight text-conditioned segmenter is precedented by VespaSeg. The contribution is the supervision, the measurement, and the bound — not the layer stack.

The honest limit on the novelty argument

Thirty-nine of these 45 works appeared in 2025 or 2026. The area is moving fast enough that a gap identified in September may be filled by December, and two of the works here (RegionE, SpotEdit) already push into region-aware efficiency from the direction we would have gone. A novelty claim should rest on the measured bound — insertion is capped at 0.212 by an object-trained prototype basis — because that is a property of the representation and will outlast any particular paper.
