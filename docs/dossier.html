What this document is

The technical report argues a result and the literature survey positions it. This document is the audit trail: every experiment run over the course of the project, in order, with what it was testing, what it returned, and whether the conclusion survived. Four entries record claims we retracted or ideas we rejected, because a project that only reports its successes cannot be checked.

1The question, in one paragraph

An instruction-guided image editor is told what to change. It is never told where. Modern editors denoise the entire frame and let the model decide implicitly, which produces two failures at once: the intended edit is applied weakly, and unrelated regions drift. The fix is to predict the edit region first and gate the editor to it. Our base paper, AdaptEdit, does exactly this — and then names, in its own limitations section, the case it cannot handle: "put a hat on the dog" where the dog has no hat. There is no source region to point at. This project builds that predictor at a scale we can actually run, and supplies the measurement AdaptEdit omits.

4What we retracted or rejected

Four entries, stated plainly. Each is here because the alternative — quietly dropping them — would make every surviving number unverifiable.

4.1A headline trained on the evaluation set

An overnight script contained cp cache_all.pt cache_train.pt, where cache_all was dev merged with train. The model was trained on the split it was then evaluated on. It surfaced because a baseline scored 0.3743 against a training-time 0.1397 — a gap far too large to be a metric difference. Retracted: the 0.1397 headline, and the claim that our supervision beat human annotation. Both were re-established later on a genuinely disjoint split.

4.2A conclusion inverted by test-set selection

An early evaluation chose the best epoch by dev IoU and concluded the spatial head was worse than the global one (0.1373 against 0.1533). Selecting on a held-out slice of train instead reverses the ordering, significantly. Test-set selection does not merely inflate results; it can flip them.

4.3Two proposed improvements that failed their own controls

We appended hand-designed free-space basis functions to the prototype set, then a geometric basis. Our standing rule is that any added basis must beat a random basis of equal dimensionality. The geometric basis beat neither the random control (+0.0035, p = 0.33) nor the plain spatial head (−0.0030, p = 0.47). Both were dropped. The gap they targeted — representing empty space — remains open and is themain subject of the next phase.

4.4A claim about insertion that we have since qualified

Experiment 08 concluded that more data would not help insertion, based on a per-kind test at roughly 500 insertion samples. With 1,828 samples insertion moved from 44% to 56% of its ceiling. The ceiling itself is real and unmoved; we were simply far below it. The surviving claim is "the basis caps insertion at 0.212", not "data does not help insertion."

7What is still open

Stated in the order we would attack them.

  1. The insertion ceiling. 0.212 is a hard bound for this basis and we reach 56% of it. Raising the bound needs a basis that spans empty space; our one attempt failed its control. This is the research problem, and it is where any novelty claim has to come from.
  2. Stale hyperparameters. Everything was tuned at 2,278 samples and reused unchanged at 8,306 so that data size was the only variable. The reported numbers are a lower bound. A re-sweep is now cheap, since roughly 10 epochs suffice rather than 60.
  3. Stage 2 recall. Our tuned mask reaches +14.2% net against the MagicBrush mask's +18.5%. Their masks are far too large yet retain more of the edit.
  4. A fair tuned baseline. CLIPSeg is evaluated at its default operating point. A like-for-like comparison would give it a held-out split to tune on too.
  5. Accuracy at 352 px is unmeasured, so the resolution-matched latency figure is a latency comparison only.
The one sentence to take away

A 4.7 M head over entirely frozen features predicts edit regions better than a 150.7 M pretrained segmenter and better than the dataset's own human annotation — and we can say exactly how much further it could go, because we measured the bound rather than guessing at it.
